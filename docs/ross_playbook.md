# Ross's Playbook

> **What this is:** the studio's memory of Ross's taste: every decision and piece of feedback he gives, distilled into principles the studio can follow. It's the guiding force for creative work, and the evidence Ross uses to decide when the studio can work on its own.
> **Keeper:** the Taste Keeper agent (logs, distills, predicts). **Authority:** Ross. Nothing here overrides a fresh instruction from him.
> **Every agent:** read the Principles before any creative or design work. If the Playbook doesn't cover something, ask Ross; don't guess.

---

## 1. Autonomy ledger

How much the studio may decide on its own, area by area. **Only Ross changes a level.** The Taste Keeper proposes a raise when the prediction record earns it.

| Level | What it means |
|---|---|
| **0: Ross decides** | Every decision goes to Ross in the sign-off format. |
| **1: Small calls** | The studio makes small calls that clearly follow the Playbook (a name, a prop, a line of dialogue), logs each one in decisions.md, and Ross can overturn any of them later. Bigger calls still go to Ross. |
| **2: Show after** | The studio drafts and finalizes whole pieces in the area (a side character, a dungeon layout), then shows Ross after. Anything that touches the main story, main cast or a Playbook principle still goes to Ross first. |

## 2. Principles

Distilled from the Feedback Log. Each cites its source entries (F#). Newest evidence wins when they conflict.

### Story & structure
- **Classic JRPG hero's journey over high-concept premises.** A hero, a call to adventure, a party gathered on the road, a world to save. The high-concept round 2 pitches were all rejected for this. (F6, F7)
- **The signature twist: the heroes aren't the first.** It's all happened before, over and over, and our heroes are the ones who break the cycle. He asked for it "in the middle of the game, followed by a huge climax" (F7), but then approved an outline with it about 7 of 10 hours in (F19, newer), so the exact timing is not a hard rule. (F7, F19)
- **Escalating scale is non-negotiable:** on foot → big robot → giant robot → giant spaceship → intergalactic god. More robots and spaceships, not fewer: round 1 was rejected partly for not delivering this. (F2, F3; carried into F7 per decisions.md)
- **Space, at least partly.** Not one planet that reaches space only at the end. (F8)
- **Shounen ladder:** the big villain is guarded by charismatic lieutenants; beat them, beat him, then reveal a greater evil, and all former foes join the heroes. (F16)
- **Once he has shaped the structure himself, he accepts what follows from it.** The three-act outline was approved in one word, with the twist at about 7 hours in (not "in the middle", F7), a 3-hour Act 1 on Harrow (F8), and the "lights out" ending all flagged and all left alone. Don't expect him to reopen the consequences of structure he already set (cast F13-F15, villain ladder F16-F17). Early evidence (one data point): treat the "twist in the middle" and "space at least partly" lines as loose intent, not hard timing rules, unless he raises them again. Still check that a draft really is built from his own choices; new studio inventions still get tweaked. (F19, R14)
- **Climaxes are conquests, not quiet resolutions.** The final fight should be a full-on beatdown that the heroes win, with the hero becoming the god in a big way. This matches his earlier "fight an intergalactic god and become god" (F2) and "conquer a greater evil" (F16), and he turned the twist draft's soft finale into a "full on beatdown" (F20). Early evidence for the finale itself (one data point); the pattern is backed by F2 and F16. He then approved the revised twist, with the five-phase beatdown and the god-vs-god finale, as is: "D2: approved" (F21). That is a second data point that the beatdown finale is what he wanted. (F2, F16, F20, F21)
- **Death and rebirth are themes, never mechanics, and never said out loud.** Understated, left for players to interpret. (F7)
- **Touchstones:** Gurren Lagann, The Matrix Reloaded, Dark Souls, Buddhist rebirth; FF7, MGS1, Fallout 3, Chrono Trigger, Chrono Cross, FF8, FF9, FFX, DQ7. Long cutscenes. Look of Mega Man Legends / Fear Effect. Inspired by, never copied. (F2)

### Tone & humor
- **Loud, goofy, rad, big-hearted. Never grimdark, never self-serious.** "Too far up its own ass" is the failure mode. His words: "dudebro awesome man totally bro righteous dude wicked awesome." (F11; the tone pass was approved in F12)
- **Villains are charismatic, each in their own way.** (F16)
- **Group and faction names sound sci-fi military and hit hard; the humor lives in the characters.** He rejected "Tenders" and "caretakers" (soft) and said "this is scifi fantasy so some of these names need to be like fucking bravo company or something" (F21). This is not a conflict with F11 ("too serious"): F11 was about the story taking itself too seriously, F21 is about what the groups are called. Read together: loud, punchy, military-sounding names, with goofy people inside them. Early evidence (one data point); the studio's earlier prediction that he would not comment on the wording was wrong (R16). Don't pick soft or cozy group names. (F11, F21)
- **The goofy tone held even inside the solemn twist.** He read the twist draft, with its sleeping crews and Red's mom, and said "tone is fine" (F20). Don't add extra solemnity or extra jokes there; keep the mix as drafted. Early evidence (one data point); it supports F11/F12. (F20)

### Characters
- **Chibi anthropomorphic animals, always.** But the story treats them with full human gravity and never makes a point of them being animals: no animal gimmicks, no "nine lives" mechanics. "Forget" they're animals in the writing, never in the art. Note: F3 ("humanoids first and foremost") could be read as "not animals"; F5 is newer and clarifies they ARE animals. (F3, F5)
- **Silent protagonist,** and a clear hero animal: dog, cat or similar. (F13)
- **Party species: cute, furry, easy to model and animate.** No tusks, long necks or legs, wings, beaks. Villains may break this only by Ross's own choice (he picked a rhino and a heron for lieutenants). Note: he said "heron no" for the party in F13 and picked a heron villain in F17 (newer); the rule is party-only, per decisions.md. (F13, F17)
- **Party-based:** a main character with a real supporting cast. (F4)
- **Names:** two leanings so far, both early evidence. He renamed the hero "Red" (short and punchy) (F15). He likes literary and nerdy-nod names: for Red's mom he asked the studio to "find some literary allegory that fits maybe some nerdy fanservice nod" (F20). He did not give the name himself, so the studio proposes and he picks. Not enough to call a rule. **Update (F21, newest):** for Red's mom he chose Option C, **Eurydice "Dee" Kincaid** (the Orpheus nod), over the studio's recommended Hesper and over Hero, even though the studio flagged that a recent hit game has a Eurydice. He picks the name that best fits the story: someone who goes into the dark to bring someone back. Story fit beat originality caution here. Early evidence (one data point). The studio should still flag originality risks, but not expect that to steer him, and since the Playbook's originality rule is about copying, a flag stays worth raising. Group and faction names are a separate leaning: see Tone & humor. (F15, F20, F21)
- **What he liked straight away:** Mox (boastful ferret kid gadgeteer who panics for four seconds, then fixes everything) and Ruo (raccoon smuggler who scores everything for style). Quirky, specific, funny characters with a sincere core. (F13)

### Art direction
- **Ross is the only artist, so practicality is taste.** Designs he can actually model and animate beat clever anatomy. (F13)
- **PSX look:** low-poly, low-res textures, Mega Man Legends / Fear Effect. (F2)

### Studio process
- **Show the work as it happens,** in plain language, with decisions at the top. (F4)
- **Momentum: he approves in a few words and expects work to carry on.** "go option a", then "option a now proceed with the acts." Don't stall after an approval; start the next step. (F1, F18)
- **Studio recommendations on process and setup have all been taken (4 of 4). On creative drafts, "approve as drafted" is usually sent back or tweaked (R3, R4, R8, R9, R11 misses; R7, R10, R12 partial).** So for creative work, expect changes, and don't present a draft as nearly final. First exception: the act outlines (R14) were approved untouched in one word, because they were built from structure he had already set (F19). Still too early to call that a pattern. The twist draft (R15), mostly new studio material, was approved with changes (a bigger finale, a name pick, a new name request), which fits "new material gets tweaked". The revised twist (R16) was approved as is: it was his own F20 changes applied back to him, the second time a draft made of his own choices got a quick yes. Even so, his name pick and a wording rejection came with it, so small details inside an approved draft still get changed. (R1-R16)
- **He likes choosing from offered alternates.** When the draft offered alternate names for the god, he picked one ("the keeper is better") (F20); the species choices worked the same way (R12 lesson). Include two or three alternates on names and other small calls. Early evidence (one more data point). Again in F21: he picked "Opt C" from four offered names, not the studio's recommendation. Offering alternates works, and the studio's recommended pick is not a safe guess for which he'll take. (F17, F20, F21)
- **Keep games separate:** circlesoft is the studio's master copy; each game gets its own repository. (F10)
- **How Ross gives feedback:** fast and blunt. He rejects at the *premise* level when a concept is off (round 1 and 2 pitches, the too-serious factions), and tweaks at the *detail* level (a species, a name) when the concept is right. When he tweaks, apply exactly what he said and don't reinterpret. (F3, F6, F7, F11, F13, F15, F17, F20, F21: a numbered list of four short answers, one per question; F21 labeled by decision, "D1: Opt C · D2: approved")
